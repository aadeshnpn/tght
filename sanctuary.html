A Permanent Meditation Sanctuary

In Spring 2028, Aadesh Neupane walks the Great Himalayan Trail High Route alone—a pilgrimage to help raise a lasting meditation sanctuary.

What the Hall Is For

A Place to Sit, Freely

The meditation hall is meant for free retreats and sitting sessions in the spirit of the Vipassana tradition—offered on a dana basis, without a fee, and open to anyone without discrimination. A quiet room where people can learn to see the mind as it is, and meet one another without a price of entry.

Why This Matters Now

Loneliness, Distress, and the Need to Gather

More than a billion people worldwide live with mental health conditions; depression alone affects about 322 million people (roughly 5.2% of adults), and anxiety disorders about 470 million.123 In the United States, about one in two adults reports loneliness; the U.S. Surgeon General has called social disconnection a public health concern whose mortality impact is similar to smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day.4

Screens keep us reachable and often leave us alone. In an age of constant feeds and accelerating AI tools, many people feel more attached to devices than to sangha—the simple company of others. Shared silence and shared practice are one answer: peer-reviewed research finds mindfulness meditation can reduce anxiety and depression symptoms,5 and mindfulness-based programs can ease psychological distress in community adults.6 A permanent hall is a place where that gathering can continue—without asking anyone to pay to sit.

  1. WHO (2025) — more than 1 billion people living with mental health conditions.
  2. WHO depression fact sheet — ~322 million people with depression in 2023; 5.2% of adults.
  3. WHO anxiety fact sheet — ~470 million people with anxiety disorders in 2023; ~5.8% of the global population.
  4. U.S. Surgeon General (2023) — ~1 in 2 U.S. adults report loneliness; mortality impact of social disconnection similar to smoking up to 15 cigarettes/day.
  5. Goyal et al., JAMA Intern Med (2014) — mindfulness meditation: moderate evidence for improved anxiety and depression vs. nonspecific active controls.
  6. Galante et al., Nature Mental Health (2023) — mindfulness-based programs reduce average psychological distress vs. passive controls (SMD −0.32).
Why Aadesh · Why MedOnMt

From Leaving Home to Offering a Seat

Aadesh grew up in Kathmandu, where meditation, spirituality, and co-existence (people of different faiths and traditions living side by side) were already in the air. Meditation was ordinary there: something people did without labeling it as a technique.

As an undergraduate at Kathmandu University, long before he ever left Nepal, he volunteered with HeNN (Help Nepal Network) on eLibrary projects that took him to remote villages far from roads, where he saw how people live, and he backpacked high Himalayan circuits. Those journeys taught him something about simplicity, silence, and how much we depend on one another.

So when he left almost everything behind in late 2016 for a Computer Science PhD, he chose Brigham Young University, in a valley ringed by peaks, on a campus where faith and practice are taken seriously, though he is not LDS himself. Far from home, the quiet Nepali way he had grown up with became a conscious way to live: not an escape, but a way to see reality as it is, and to notice how widely people suffer.

Around 2019 he began offering dharma talks at a local Provo sangha. People arrived seeking relief and a little connection; when the practice helped, word of mouth did the rest. Friends brought friends, and many returned. More than four hundred unique people have sat with those talks over the years.

Out of that same need, he started the annual MedOnMt mountain meditation retreat in 2022; more than thirty people have joined those wilderness gatherings so far. He also leads meditation at the Walk for Peace series at the Utah State Capitol in Salt Lake City.

That quiet demand is why a permanent free hall feels like the natural next step—a dana-based home for this work, open to anyone who wants to sit. Current retreats and gatherings live on medonmt.org and the retreats page; more of Aadesh’s story is on his page.

The Place

Below a Mountain

The sanctuary is envisioned most likely below a mountain: a main house, two guest houses, and a meditation hall, designed to be self-sustaining. Name and exact location are still being discerned.

Along the Way

Join a stretch of the pilgrimage

MedOnMt’s Great Himalaya Trail journey is a fundraising pilgrimage for a free meditation sanctuary. Along the way, a few short sections will open to anyone who has the fitness, the schedule, a love of the mountains, and a real eagerness for this cause — and who wants to walk the path for themselves.

About 2–4 people can join each stretch, with roughly 20–30 across the whole pilgrimage, so the experience stays quiet and personal. One example: a few days through the Khumbu valley. You cover your own travel, permits, gear, food, and insurance. You simply join for a firsthand taste of the pilgrimage.

How to apply for those seats will be shared later. If that kind of walk calls to you, watch this space — or reach out at aadesh@medonmt.org.
